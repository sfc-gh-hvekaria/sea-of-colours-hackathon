00 Start here

A plain-English walkthrough of how an AI takes a turn in this game, for someone who has never wired a language model into anything. No prior knowledge assumed. Everything after this section goes deeper on one piece of it.

What the "AI" actually is

It is a frontier model — the same kind of general-purpose language model behind a chat assistant. It was trained on text from the open world. It has never seen this game, has no idea it is playing one, and cannot look at anything. It has exactly one ability: you give it text, and it gives you text back.

That is a strange thing to put in charge of a strategy game, so the interesting engineering is not the model at all. It is the layer that translates a live game into words the model can reason about, and translates its answer back into legal moves. That layer is called the harness, and it is ordinary Python.

Three parties, and what passes between them

Everything crossing those arrows is text. The model is stateless — it remembers nothing between turns, cannot call functions, cannot query the game, and never touches the board. If it is not written into the words the harness sends, the model does not know it.

The whole turn, in one line

Blue is the game engine, green is our Python, amber is the model. That colour code holds for the rest of the guide. Click any box to jump to it.

The same thing, slowly

Now the long version. Each step below says who is acting, in their colour, and shows the real thing that step produced on the night this whole guide takes apart.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

The model is one box in a nine-box loop, and it never touches the game. It is handed a carefully written document and asked to pick from a list. The skill in building an agent like this is almost entirely in the two green boxes either side of it — deciding what it gets to know, and guaranteeing that whatever it answers turns into a legal move.

01 It is all text

There is no vision model, no board renderer, no grid of pixels anywhere in the agent. Once a night, a Python program writes a long plain-text document, sends it to a language model, and reads a short reply. Everything the agent believes, wants and decides passes through those two pieces of text.

This guide takes apart one real night — day 4 of a 7-day season, seat p1, playing against one opponent. It is a good specimen because most of the machinery fires at once: p1 has found a pure seam of its own, a rival has broadcast a second one across the map, and there are two harvesters and three probes to spend on both.

Look at the shape of that first number. The document handed to the model is about eighty-five thousand characters, and here is how it divides up.

Almost none of it is the board. The rules and the doctrine — how the game works and how it ought to be played — take about three fifths of the page, and they are close to identical every night. The next largest block is the menu of plays the harness has already worked out and priced. The state of the world, the thing that actually changed since yesterday, is roughly a tenth of what gets sent; the memory of last night is smaller still, and the question itself is barely one percent.

02 One night, end to end

The night is a pipeline. The engine hands the harness a fogged view of the board; the harness turns that into a document; the model reads it twice, once to reason and once to commit; a compiler turns the commitment into legal moves; and the moves go back to the engine. Click any stage to see what it does and which file owns it.

Two passes, not one

The model is asked twice. The first pass — THINK — is prose only: no JSON, no commitment, just reasoning about what is on the board and what the night is for. The second pass — PLAN — receives that reasoning back and must answer in strict JSON with the options it is choosing.

Splitting them is what stops the two jobs interfering. A model asked to reason and emit schema-valid JSON in one breath tends to do both badly: the reasoning gets clipped to fit the format, and the format gets broken by the reasoning. Here the THINK pass has room to be wrong out loud, and the PLAN pass has almost nothing to do except decide.

03 What it can actually see

This is the board on the night in question — first as the engine holds it, then as p1 receives it. The gap between the two is the whole game.

ENGINE TRUTH

Vision is a set of circles

Live sight is not a radius around your units — harvesters are blind. It is exactly the union of your probes' vision disks, and a probe covers a Euclidean radius of four. On this night p1 has one probe still standing, at (27,17), with one night of life left in it. That single disk is the entirety of p1's live vision: 49 cells out of 1,120.

Everything outside it falls into one of two states. Echo is ground this seat has seen before and no longer watches — exact coordinates, but a reading that could be days stale and may since have been stripped by someone else. Fog is ground it has never seen at all. Both are still walkable; you simply move through them without knowing what you are stepping on.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

Probes buy sight, and sight is scarce and expiring. Most of what looks like a bad decision by the agent turns out, on inspection, to be a reasonable decision made inside one small circle.

04 What it knows but cannot see

If sight were all there was, the game would be a slow crawl behind a probe. It is not, because of one broadcast rule: when any house finds a cell of pure red, everybody is told. That single mechanic is what makes the game a race rather than a parallel solitaire.

But the broadcast is deliberately blurred. You are given a smear of weighted cells and an off-centre point, never the square. On this night there are two live signs — p1's own, and the rival's forty cells away. Below, the smear each one broadcasts is drawn over the true position of the pure it is pointing at.

THE SMEAR

Note how far the broadcast centre sits from the actual pure. That offset is not noise in the data — it is the design. A sign tells every seat roughly where to race; it does not tell anyone where to land.

How the agent is told about it

All of this arrives as a block of text under the world view, and the wording works hard to keep the two kinds of knowledge apart, because confusing them is expensive: treating a smear as a coordinate means dropping a harvester on empty ground.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

A sign gives you an area and a deadline. An echo gives you an exact square and a doubt. The agent's hardest decisions are almost all about which of those two to act on tonight.

05 The value pyramid

Before the model is asked anything, the harness ranks everything worth having on the board. This is the value pyramid, and its job is to guarantee that the obvious play is always on the menu.

It exists because of a specific failure. An earlier version could see a pure cell and offer no way to take it: the pure was not wrapped in a redsign pattern, so it fell through every precondition-gated branch in the option machinery and the night banked nothing. Value and reachability were being computed implicitly across five modules with no shared model. So the top of the pyramid was made explicit and, importantly, unconditional:

The ranking is by provenance as much as by worth. Live means a probe is on it right now. Echo means this seat saw it and looked away — the engine tags each cell's freshness directly, so this is read, not guessed. Expected means nobody has seen it and only a sign suggests it is there.

A live pure outranks an echo pure because nobody can have taken it behind your back. An echo pure outranks visible mass because 255 at the pure multiplier is worth 765 points, and no amount of mass on a six-cell walk catches that. Below those, it is ordinary arithmetic.

What it produced on this night

p1's own pure at (31,18) was in echo — seen on day 3, now outside the probe disk — and there was a live cell adjacent enough to walk in from. That is tier two, so the pyramid forced a walk-in grab onto the menu whatever else the seam machinery decided to offer.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

The pyramid is not advice to the model — it is a floor under the menu. Its promise is that if a pure is takeable tonight, some option on the card takes it, no matter which other subsystem did or did not fire.

06 The option menu

This is the hinge of the whole design. The model is never asked "where should the harvester go?". It is handed a numbered list of fully-formed plays, each with its route already drawn and priced, and asked which ones to run and in what order. It answers with identifiers. It never composes a coordinate.

The reason is reliability. A language model asked for coordinates will occasionally produce a beautiful plan that walks through a wall, off the map, or onto ground it cannot legally drop on — and the failure is silent, because the plan reads perfectly. Precomputing the geometry moves that entire class of error out of the model's reach. What is left for the model is the part it is actually good at: weighing a sure thing against a gamble.

The thirteen plays offered on this night

Click any option to open it. The three highlighted in green are the ones the model went on to choose.

What each option carries

Every entry is priced before the model sees it: the exact walk, the expected yield broken down by tier, whether any of that yield is echo rather than live, whether it double-counts a cell another option also claims, what it crushes, and a collision-risk note that names who else can reach it.

The double-count warning matters more than it looks. Four separate options on this card end on the same pure at (31,18). Their yields cannot be added — the first one there banks it and the rest arrive at stripped green worth -100 a cell. Saying so explicitly is cheaper than hoping the model notices.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

The menu is authoritative for what is legal, but it is not the whole board — it deliberately does not surface everything the world view and the signs contain. The model is told to use those to choose between options and to trim them, never to invent new ones.

07 The card

Everything so far has been a piece of one document. This is the document. The harness calls it the card: one page per turn holding exactly what the model was given, what it wrote back, and what that compiled to.

The card exists because a season night happens once. The audit table truncates the stored prompt at 32 KB, which on a card this size does not even reach the option menu, and the board cannot be reconstructed afterwards without replaying the whole season. Without the card, a bad decision is unexaminable — you can see what the agent did but never what it was looking at when it decided.

What is on it

The proportions are worth dwelling on. The rules and doctrine are nearly static — the same text most nights, and the place where strategy gets taught. The board and the memory are what change nightly, and together they are about a sixth of the page. The option menu, sitting physically inside the last-night section, is bigger than both of them combined, because it carries thirteen fully-priced plays.

The question at the end is 1% of the document. By the time the model reaches it, the work of framing the decision has already been done.

08 Thinking, then committing

Here is what came back. On the left, the reasoning pass, in the model's own words. On the right, the JSON it committed to afterwards. This is the entire output of the agent for the night — a few hundred words and one object.

What the reasoning is doing

Read the THINK pass and you can see it working through the same structure the card laid out. It reflects on last night and correctly attributes the probe losses to the EMP. It reads both signs, tags its own with the doctrine's label — CASE 1, mine=true — and notes that the rival's at (17,6) is fogged to it but lit by their probe at (16,9). It counts its remaining vision: one disk, one night left. Then it makes the call that decides the turn: secure my own pure first, then attack theirs with the second harvester.

That ordering is not incidental. Both signs are public, so both seats know about both pures, and the seat that arrives first at a cell takes it while the second arrives at stripped ground worth -100. Tempo is the currency, and the model is explicitly reasoning about it.

What the commitment contains

Note that plan is just three strings. That is the entire instruction to the game — no coordinates, no hours, no unit assignments. The rest of the JSON is bookkeeping: a posture, a read of the situation, and two pieces of prose written for the agent's own future self, which is what section 11 is about.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

The model's job is compressed to almost nothing: pick three identifiers from a menu of thirteen and put them in order. Everything that makes that a good three came before it, in how the menu was built and priced.

09 The packager

Three identifiers go in. Fifteen concrete moves come out. The packager is the compiler between the model's intent and the engine's wire format, and the most important thing about it is what it is not allowed to do.

Legality, not authorship

The packager may refuse anything the engine would reject: a drop on a cell with no live coverage, a step onto ground known to be stripped, a run for a harvester that does not exist, a probe when the stock is empty, a walk past the hold cap, more than 21 moves. It also reorders runs so that a probe bought to make a drop legal lands before the drop that needs it.

It must not shorten a route because the route looks dangerous, swap a target it disagrees with, or quietly drop a play it thinks is unwise. When it disagrees, it reports and keeps.

That line is load-bearing rather than stylistic. A compiler that silently edits plans makes the agent's reasoning unfalsifiable: you can no longer tell a bad decision from a good decision that was overwritten, so every subsequent diagnosis is built on sand. This constraint was learned the hard way — earlier versions did rewrite plans, and the debugging cost was severe.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

The division of labour is strict. The agent chooses what; the packager guarantees legal execution and narrates any gap between the two. Neither is allowed to do the other's job.

10 Reaching the engine

The moves leave the harness as one JSON array through a single call, and that is the last the agent has to do with the night. Everything below is the engine's business.

Four verbs, and that is the entire vocabulary. drop puts a harvester on a cell — legal only where you have live coverage. step moves it one cell and harvests whatever it lands on, legal anywhere including fog. pickup lifts it back to orbit with its cargo. probe drops an eye from orbit onto any cell at all.

Note the asymmetry, because most of the agent's cleverness lives in it: you must be able to see where you land, but not where you walk. That single rule is what makes a blind walk into fog possible, and it is the basis of every attack on a seam nobody can see.

11 Memory

Each night is a fresh model call with no memory of the last one. Continuity is not something the model has — it is something the harness manufactures, by writing the night down and reading it back tomorrow.

The mechanism is deliberately not "let the agent take notes". It is adversarial: the agent's own claim about what it would do is stored, and then placed next to the engine's record of what actually happened. The agent is then made to reconcile the two.

What last night looked like on this card

This is the block that greeted the agent on day 4. Its own intent from day 3 in quotes, the orders it gave, and then the engine's per-hour truth — which is read back out of the stored replay frames, not reconstructed from the plan.

Everything after EXECUTION LOG is engine testimony. That is what makes the block hard to argue with, and it is why the instruction above it reads never claim a harvest that is not listed there. An agent grading its own homework will report a clean night after a disaster; an agent handed the hour log has to account for the gap.

Notice what the log surfaces on this night: two probes lost, one to an EMP at hour 1 and one to expiry. That is why the agent enters day 4 with a single disk of vision, and it is the fact that shapes the whole turn.

The running thread

Beside the nightly block sits the journal — one compressed entry per night, carrying the intent, the outcome, what was seen, and the reflection. It is the agent's only long-term memory, and it is short by design.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

Memory here is a control, not a convenience. Pairing the agent's stated intent with the engine's record is what stops a season drifting into confident fiction — and it is why nearly every bug in this agent was found by reading a card rather than a stack trace.

12 Where the Python lives

All of the above is one package: sea_of_colours/orchestrator_2/harnesses/tabula_v12/. Modules marked in green left something on the card this guide has been taking apart, and the mark says what to point at. The unmarked ones are part of the harness but produced nothing identifiable on this particular night.

The engine boundary

The rule that keeps all of this honest is narrow and worth stating exactly. The harness reads game state through one function and writes it through two:

Nothing else in the package touches a session. No module mutates state as a side effect of computing an option, and none reaches past build_agent_view for information the seat has not earned. It would be easy to do — the engine is right there in the same process — and it would invalidate every game the agent has played.

Constants are imported rather than copied for the same reason. Probe radius, hold capacity, weapon dials and the day cap all come from game/tuning.py, game/session.py and game/weapons.py, so a retune reaches the agent's prompts without anyone remembering to update them. The full contract is written down in ENGINE_INTERFACE.md next to the package.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

One way in, two ways out, no shortcuts. The agent plays the same game as a human at the same screen, with the same fog — which is the only reason its results mean anything.
